# MEGDA Legislative Redline 04

## Article 86 → Proposed Article 86a
### Effective Human Review and Contestation of High-Impact AI Decisions

**Status:** Working Draft v0.1  
**Project:** MEGDA — Model for European Governance of Democratic AI  
**Primary legal anchors:** Regulation (EU) 2024/1689, Articles 14, 26, 79, 85 and 86  
**Related MEGDA provisions:** proposed Articles 27a, 27b and 84a  
**Preferred legislative technique:** retain Articles 85 and 86 and insert a new Article 86a immediately after Article 86, with consequential clarifications to Articles 14 and 26.

---

## 1. Current legal anchors

### Article 14 — Human oversight
Requires high-risk AI systems to be designed and developed so that they can be effectively overseen by natural persons during use.

### Article 26 — Deployer obligations
Requires deployers to assign human oversight to persons with the necessary competence, training and authority.

### Article 85 — Right to lodge a complaint
Allows any natural or legal person having grounds to consider that the AI Act has been infringed to submit a complaint to the relevant market-surveillance authority.

### Article 86 — Right to explanation
Under defined conditions, gives affected persons the right to obtain clear and meaningful explanations of the role of certain high-risk AI systems in individual decision-making.

### Article 79 — Risk intervention
Provides the market-surveillance procedure for AI systems presenting a risk.

---

## 2. MEGDA legal gap

MEGDA identifies six gaps:

1. Human oversight is a system/deployer obligation, not always an individual procedural right.
2. Explanation does not equal reconsideration.
3. A complaint to a regulator does not necessarily correct an individual decision.
4. Human review can be nominal if the reviewer lacks authority, time or information.
5. Delay can defeat the remedy.
6. Reviewability should follow the exercise of power, not merely product classification.

---

# Article 86a
## Effective human review and contestation of high-impact AI decisions

### 1. Right to effective human review

Any natural person materially affected by a decision, recommendation, ranking, classification, prioritisation or other determination in which an AI system used in a deployment classified as having a **high democratic impact** under Article 27a has materially influenced the outcome shall have the right to obtain effective human review where the outcome:

(a) produces legal effects concerning that person;

(b) similarly significantly affects that person's rights, legitimate interests or access to an essential service or opportunity; or

(c) creates a reasonably foreseeable risk of serious harm to that person.

This right shall apply without prejudice to more protective rights available under Union or national law.

### 2. Meaning of effective human review

Review shall be considered effective only where the natural person performing it:

(a) has the competence and training necessary to understand the relevant role, capabilities and limitations of the AI system;

(b) has sufficient authority to depart from, disregard, reverse or modify the AI-influenced outcome;

(c) has access to the information reasonably necessary to reconsider the matter;

(d) is able to consider additional information, context or evidence submitted by the affected person;

(e) is not required, formally or in practice, to treat the AI output as presumptively correct merely because it was produced by the system;

(f) has sufficient time and organisational support to conduct a genuine review;

(g) can identify and record the grounds on which the reviewed decision is maintained, modified or reversed.

A purely formal confirmation of an AI output shall not constitute effective human review.

### 3. Notice of AI influence

At or before the time a materially affected person is informed of an outcome falling within paragraph 1, that person shall be informed, in clear and accessible language:

(a) that an AI system materially influenced the outcome;

(b) of the purpose for which the AI system was used;

(c) of the identity or institutional function of the deployer responsible for the decision;

(d) of the right to obtain an explanation where Article 86 or other applicable law so provides;

(e) of the right to request effective human review under this Article;

(f) of the procedure and time limit for requesting review;

(g) of any available complaint, administrative or judicial remedy.

### 4. Request for review

The deployer shall provide a procedure that permits the affected person to request review without disproportionate burden.

The procedure shall:

(a) be accessible through at least one non-digital channel where exclusively digital access would create a material barrier;

(b) not require the affected person to identify a specific technical defect in the AI system;

(c) permit the person to provide contextual information or evidence relevant to the decision;

(d) acknowledge receipt without undue delay;

(e) identify the body or function responsible for review;

(f) state the expected timetable.

No fee shall be charged for the first review of a materially adverse decision under this Article.

### 5. Interim protection

Where immediate execution of the contested decision could create a serious risk of irreversible or disproportionate harm, the deployer shall assess without undue delay whether temporary suspension, preservation of the status quo or another proportionate interim measure is necessary pending review.

The existence of a request for review shall not automatically suspend every decision.

Any refusal to adopt an interim measure in a case presenting a plausible risk of serious or irreversible harm shall be reasoned.

### 6. Time limits

Human review shall be completed without undue delay and within a period proportionate to the significance and urgency of the decision.

The Commission shall establish common maximum procedural benchmarks distinguishing, where appropriate, between:

(a) urgent cases involving health, personal safety, subsistence or comparable risks of serious and immediate harm;

(b) ordinary cases involving significant legal or practical effects;

(c) complex cases requiring additional evidence or coordination.

Where a benchmark cannot be met, the deployer shall explain the delay and provide a revised timetable.

Administrative complexity arising from the deployer's choice to use AI shall not, by itself, justify excessive delay.

### 7. Outcome of review

Following effective human review, the affected person shall receive a reasoned outcome stating:

(a) whether the original decision is maintained, modified or reversed;

(b) the principal factual and legal reasons;

(c) how material information submitted by the affected person was considered;

(d) the role, if any, that the AI system continued to play;

(e) available complaint, administrative or judicial remedies.

### 8. No retaliation or adverse inference

A person shall not be subjected to retaliation, disadvantage or adverse inference solely because that person requested an explanation, requested human review, lodged a complaint or exercised another remedy under Union or national law.

### 9. Relationship with Article 85 complaints

A request for human review and a complaint under Article 85 shall serve distinct purposes.

Human review concerns reconsideration of an outcome affecting the person.

A complaint under Article 85 concerns a possible infringement of the Regulation and may support supervisory or enforcement action.

Use of one route shall not, by itself, prevent use of the other.

### 10. Collective pattern escalation

Where substantially similar review requests reveal a recurring pattern of materially adverse outcomes, affected persons, representative organisations, workers' representatives or qualified civil-society organisations may request that the competent authority examine whether the issue is systemic.

The competent authority shall assess whether the pattern warrants:

(a) additional monitoring;

(b) supplementary scientific verification under Article 84a;

(c) reconsideration of the democratic impact assessment under Article 27a;

(d) investigation under Article 79 or another applicable supervisory procedure.

### 11. Logging and auditability of review

The deployer shall maintain records sufficient to demonstrate that the review procedure is effective.

Those records shall include, where appropriate:

(a) number and type of review requests;

(b) median and maximum response times;

(c) proportion of decisions maintained, modified or reversed;

(d) recurring grounds for modification or reversal;

(e) material disagreement between human reviewers and AI outputs;

(f) identified failures of human-oversight arrangements.

For deployments of high democratic impact, a non-confidential summary of review effectiveness shall be included in periodic public accountability information.

### 12. Organisational independence and reviewer support

Deployers shall organise review functions so that reviewers are not subject to incentives that materially discourage justified departure from AI outputs.

Reviewers shall receive appropriate training, access to relevant information, adequate time, escalation routes and protection for independent professional judgment exercised in good faith.

### 13. Accessible and inclusive review

Review procedures shall take reasonable account of accessibility requirements, disability, language barriers and the circumstances of persons in positions of vulnerability.

Use of AI to assist administration of the review process shall not result in the contested outcome being effectively reconsidered only by another automated process.

### 14. Relationship with other Union law

This Article shall be without prejudice to rights and remedies under other Union law, including data-protection, consumer-protection, equality, labour, administrative-procedure and sector-specific legislation.

Where another applicable Union-law instrument provides a higher level of protection, the more protective rule shall apply.

Nothing in this Article shall be interpreted as limiting the right to an effective remedy and to a fair hearing under the Charter of Fundamental Rights of the European Union.

### 15. Common procedural standards

The Commission, with the support of the AI Office and European Artificial Intelligence Board and after consultation with relevant fundamental-rights bodies, civil-society organisations, workers' representatives and sectoral authorities, shall develop common procedural standards for effective human review.

Those standards shall address at least:

- reviewer competence and authority;
- independence from automation bias;
- accessibility;
- procedural timelines;
- interim protection;
- reason-giving;
- documentation;
- escalation of systemic patterns;
- interaction with Article 85 complaints;
- metrics for assessing whether human review is genuine rather than formal.

---

## 3. Consequential clarification to Article 14

Where Article 86a applies, human-oversight measures should be designed so that designated natural persons can effectively exercise the review powers required by that Article.

The purpose is to connect **system design** with **procedural remedy**.

---

## 4. Consequential clarification to Article 26

For deployments subject to Article 86a, the deployer should ensure that persons assigned to human review have:

- actual authority;
- appropriate access to relevant information;
- sufficient organisational independence;
- sufficient time and support.

---

## 5. Why a new Article 86a?

Article 85 creates a route to complain about infringements.

Article 86 creates a route to understand certain AI-influenced decisions.

Article 86a would create the missing third element:

> **a route to have the decision genuinely reconsidered by a responsible human actor.**

### Complaint
**Was the Regulation infringed?**

### Explanation
**How did AI contribute to this decision?**

### Human review
**Should this decision remain in force when a responsible human re-examines my case?**

---

## 6. The Legal Key

The Legal Key asks:

> **Is this exercise of algorithmic power lawful, attributable to a responsible actor and subject to an effective remedy?**

Core principle:

> **No significant AI-mediated exercise of power should become legally unanswerable because responsibility has been dispersed between a model, a provider, a deployer and an institution.**

---

## 7. Relationship to the three-key architecture

### Scientific Key
**Evidence**  
Do the material technical claims survive independent verification?

### Legal Key
**Responsibility and remedy**  
Is there an identifiable responsible actor, a lawful basis for the use, meaningful explanation where applicable, and an effective route to challenge the outcome?

### Democratic Key
**Legitimacy**  
Is the exercise of algorithmic power proportionate, publicly contestable and legitimate in its social context?

---

## 8. Anti-capture principle

The Legal Key must not be reduced to provider-defined internal appeals.

MEGDA therefore distinguishes between:

- the provider that develops the system;
- the deployer that makes or implements the decision;
- the human reviewer who can reconsider the outcome;
- the market-surveillance authority that investigates regulatory infringement;
- the courts or other competent bodies that provide external legal remedy.

No contractual allocation of responsibility should force the affected person to discover which actor in the AI value chain is legally answerable.

---

## 9. Legal and institutional route

### Preferred legal vehicle

Amend Regulation (EU) 2024/1689 by inserting Article 86a in Chapter IX, Section 4 — Remedies, with consequential clarifications to Articles 14 and 26.

### Existing legal footholds

- Article 14 establishes effective human oversight.
- Article 26 requires deployers to assign competent, trained and authorised natural persons to oversight.
- Article 85 creates a right to complain about infringements.
- Article 86 creates a right to explanation in defined circumstances.
- Article 79 provides the system-level route for regulatory intervention where AI systems present a risk.

### Primary institutional targets

1. European Commission / AI Office
2. European Parliament — LIBE and IMCO
3. Council of the European Union / Member States
4. European AI Board
5. national market-surveillance authorities
6. data-protection, equality, labour, consumer and sectoral authorities
7. courts and administrative-review bodies
