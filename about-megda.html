Model for European Governance of Democratic AI

What is MEGDA?

MEGDA is a policy-design proposal for using artificial intelligence to improve public decision-making without allowing either machines or institutions to become unaccountable centres of power. It starts from the belief that AI can reduce administrative friction, help institutions deal with complexity and make democratic processes faster — while final responsibility remains human, visible and contestable.

AI should reduce friction. Use machines to process complexity, duplication and information overload.
Efficiency is not legitimacy. A faster answer is not automatically a lawful, fair or democratic decision.
Power must remain separated. No single actor should build, verify, authorise and judge challenges to the same high-impact system.
30 seconds

MEGDA in one idea

Many public systems are not slow because every delay protects a right. They are often slow because information is fragmented, procedures are repetitive, institutions do not communicate efficiently and large quantities of evidence are difficult for human beings to process in reasonable time.

Artificial intelligence can help reduce that friction. But if the same technology is introduced without democratic safeguards, it can also make decisions harder to contest and concentrate institutional power.

The purpose is not to place democracy under technological control. It is to give democracy better tools for governing complexity without surrendering human responsibility.
3 minutes

The underlying idea

01 — COMPLEXITY

Complexity is not protection.

A process that takes months is not automatically more democratic because it is slow. Where delay comes from fragmented information, duplication or administrative opacity, AI can shorten the process without removing legal safeguards.

02 — EFFICIENCY

Efficiency is not legitimacy.

A system that produces an answer in seconds should not automatically be entitled to make the decision. Speed does not replace rights, responsibility, context or political judgment.

03 — POWER

Technological power should be separated.

The actor that builds an AI system should not also be the only actor able to verify it, authorise it, interpret its effects and decide whether challenges against it are valid.

Human + machine

Cooperation, not substitution

MEGDA does not begin from the question “How do we control AI?”. It begins from a different question: how do we prevent AI from becoming another instrument through which power becomes concentrated, opaque and difficult to contest?

Machines can help with

  • processing large volumes of information;
  • comparing documents, rules and evidence;
  • detecting recurring patterns and inconsistencies;
  • translating and organising public contributions;
  • supporting monitoring and procedural coordination;
  • reducing the cognitive burden of complex administrative systems.

Humans remain responsible for

  • determining acceptable social trade-offs;
  • interpreting rights and legal responsibility;
  • evaluating consequences in context;
  • preserving minority and dissenting positions;
  • deciding when a decision should be reversed;
  • being publicly answerable for the exercise of power.
Automate administrative complexity where possible. Do not automate political responsibility.
The architecture

Three questions, three independent keys

For high-impact uses, MEGDA separates technical evidence, legal responsibility and democratic legitimacy. The keys are complementary, but they are not interchangeable.

Democratic Key

Is this use legitimate?

Who is affected? Is the use proportionate? Were affected communities meaningfully heard? Can the decision be publicly contested?

Scientific Key

Does the evidence hold?

Are the material technical claims independently verified, sufficiently representative and reproducible under relevant conditions?

Legal Key

Who is responsible?

Is the use lawful, attributable to identifiable actors and subject to meaningful explanation, human review, complaint and remedy?

Why democracy?

Public institutions are not automatically self-correcting

MEGDA does not assume that an institution is sufficiently democratic simply because it is public. Contestability must be built into the architecture itself.

Citizens can raise challenges. Independent scientists can question technical claims. Human reviewers can overturn AI-mediated decisions. Authorities must explain why systems continue operating. Serious new signals can reopen an authorisation that was previously granted.

The objective is not permanent institutional suspicion. It is to ensure that no important exercise of algorithmic power becomes invisible, unanswerable or effectively irreversible.

What MEGDA is not

A governance model should also define its limits

Not AI governing citizens. AI supports processes; it does not acquire democratic sovereignty.
Not a central authority controlling all AI. MEGDA deliberately separates oversight functions.
Not continuous surveillance. Accountability does not require turning institutions or citizens into permanent objects of monitoring.
Not rule by experts. Scientific verification cannot substitute democratic legitimacy or legal responsibility.
Not mass consultation on every decision. Participation should be proportional to the impact and nature of the use.
Not a claim of finality. MEGDA is a policy-design proposal intended to be criticised, stress-tested and improved.
Why now?

Better tools for institutions facing more complexity

Administrative complexity can protect rights when it creates due process, evidence and accountability. But complexity can also become simple friction: duplicated requests, unreadable documentation, disconnected databases, repetitive checks and delays that make responsibility harder rather than easier to locate.

MEGDA starts from the possibility that human institutions and machine capabilities can complement each other. AI can help make complex systems more legible and faster; democratic governance can ensure that this new capacity does not become a new form of coercion.

AI-assisted deliberation shall reduce informational complexity, not political plurality.
